**The feeling that you still need to go after a bowel movement — the clinical name is tenesmus — most often traces back to one of three things: hard, slow stool from constipation; irritable bowel syndrome, especially the constipation-predominant subtype; or a pelvic floor that pushes against itself instead of relaxing.** A single off day is nothing. The sensation showing up most days for weeks is a signal — one that responds well to the right test and the right fix, which is rarely more fibre.

## Key takeaways

- **Tenesmus is a symptom, not a diagnosis.** It's the persistent feeling of needing to pass stool even when the rectum is empty ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/24107-tenesmus)).
- **Three causes dominate**: constipation with hard, impacted stool; irritable bowel syndrome; and pelvic floor dyssynergia, where the muscles that should relax instead tighten during a push.
- **Up to 30% of people with ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease** report tenesmus, so new or persistent incomplete evacuation after age 40, or with any bleeding, needs a doctor rather than another laxative ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/24107-tenesmus)).
- **Pelvic floor biofeedback is the single most effective treatment** for dyssynergic defecation — one 109-patient randomised trial reported 80% major improvement at 6 months versus 22% on polyethylene glycol, with the benefit holding for two years ([Chiarioni et al., *Gastroenterology*, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16530506/)).
- **Red flags that override everything**: rectal bleeding, unintentional weight loss, fever, severe pain, or new-onset symptoms after age 50 ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003131.htm)).

## What "incomplete evacuation" actually means

Doctors call it **tenesmus**: a constant feeling that you need to go to the bathroom, but you can't ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/24107-tenesmus)). Day-to-day, people describe it as:

- Finishing a bowel movement and still feeling full
- Returning to the toilet 10–30 minutes later, often with little result
- A dull pressure or urge in the rectum that doesn't resolve after passing stool
- Straining repeatedly and getting only small pellets or ribbons out

The rectum is often not actually full. The sensation can come from nerves in the rectal area signalling that something is there, from an inflamed rectal wall, or from muscles that didn't fully coordinate the push. The pattern around it tells you which.

## The four most common causes

### 1. Constipation and stool impaction

Hard, dry stool stuck low in the rectum keeps triggering the "I need to go" reflex even after a partial pass. The Cleveland Clinic flags this directly: hard, impacted poop stuck in the bowel can irritate it, making it constantly want to evacuate ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/24107-tenesmus)). The giveaway is Bristol Type 1 or 2 stool — see the full chart in our [Bristol Stool Scale guide](/poopcheck-blog/bristol-stool-chart-types-explained/) — plus straining and fewer than three bowel movements a week. Our [constipation remedies guide](/poopcheck-blog/constipation-causes-and-remedies/) walks through the fiber-fluid-PEG ladder most of this responds to.

### 2. Irritable bowel syndrome (especially IBS-C and IBS-M)

Rome IV lists the sense of incomplete evacuation as one of the six core symptoms of **functional constipation**, and it shows up frequently in **IBS with constipation (IBS-C)** and the **mixed subtype (IBS-M)**. In IBS the problem is less about blockage and more about **visceral hypersensitivity** — the gut's nerves fire louder than they should, so even a small amount of stool in the rectum feels like a full one. Stress and anxiety reliably amplify it. If stickiness, mucus, or [alternating constipation and diarrhea](/poopcheck-blog/alternating-constipation-diarrhea/) is in the picture too, the IBS pattern is likely the right frame.

### 3. Pelvic floor dyssynergia (the under-diagnosed one)

This is the cause that most people — and many primary-care providers — miss. During a normal push, the puborectalis muscle and the external anal sphincter *relax* so stool can exit. In **dyssynergic defecation (DD)**, they contract instead, or fail to relax enough. The result is straining against your own closed door. A review in a leading gastroenterology journal reports that **a feeling of incomplete evacuation is present in 76% of DD patients** ([Rao, *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* review, PMC4000585](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4000585/)). Slow transit constipation coexists in roughly two-thirds of them, which is why "add more fibre" so often backfires: it loads the proximal colon without fixing the mechanical bottleneck at the bottom.

The clinical hints that point to DD rather than ordinary constipation:

- Needing to press on the perineum, the vaginal wall, or the lower abdomen to get stool out
- Prolonged straining with little result
- A clear sensation of a "trap door" that won't open
- Normal fibre and water intake with persistent symptoms
- Symptoms that worsen with laxative dose escalation rather than resolve

If you're not sure whether your stool shape is actually changing from one day to the next, [log a photo with PoopCheck](/download/) each time and let the AI classify Bristol type and consistency against your own baseline — a dated record of pellet days versus normal days is far more useful to a GI consult than "it's been off lately."

### 4. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)

Ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease inflame the rectal and colonic wall, which keeps the urge-to-defecate signal firing even on an empty rectum. **Up to 30% of people with UC or Crohn's experience some tenesmus**, and the Cleveland Clinic names IBD as the single most common cause of rectal tenesmus overall ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/24107-tenesmus)). Blood in the stool, [mucus discharge](/poopcheck-blog/mucus-in-stool/), weight loss, or a new fever alongside the sensation is the pattern that pushes IBD up the list.

MedlinePlus lists the full differential: anorectal abscess, colorectal cancer or tumors, Crohn disease, infectious colitis, radiation proctitis, ulcerative colitis, and bowel motility disorders ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003131.htm)). None of those is diagnosable from the sensation alone.

## How a doctor sorts it out

A sensible workup starts simple and escalates:

1. **History and digital rectal exam.** A trained examiner can feel paradoxical contraction of the pelvic floor on simulated defecation. That exam alone catches a sizeable fraction of DD cases.
2. **Anorectal manometry + balloon expulsion test.** The standard functional workup. A balloon-expulsion time over one minute is strongly suggestive of DD.
3. **Defecography (MR or fluoroscopic).** Imaging while you push — useful when rectocele, intussusception, or rectal prolapse is on the differential.
4. **Flexible sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy.** When bleeding, weight loss, or new symptoms after 50 are on the table — to rule out IBD, [pencil-thin stools](/poopcheck-blog/pencil-thin-stools-when-to-worry/) from a stricture, or colorectal cancer.

Rome IV formally defines a **functional defecation disorder** as functional constipation (or IBS-C) *plus* objective evidence of impaired evacuation on two of three of those physiologic tests — so the diagnosis is as much a test result as a symptom cluster.

## What actually fixes it

The fix depends on the mechanism, which is why the testing matters.

- **If the cause is slow, hard stool**: climb the standard ladder — 25–38 g of fibre a day, 2–3 L of fluid, movement, then PEG (polyethylene glycol / MiraLAX) as the first OTC laxative. Our [constipation remedies post](/poopcheck-blog/constipation-causes-and-remedies/) covers doses and timing.
- **If the cause is dyssynergic defecation**: biofeedback, not more laxatives. The Chiarioni 2006 trial randomised 109 patients with pelvic floor dyssynergia to five weekly biofeedback sessions versus polyethylene glycol: **80% of the biofeedback arm reported major improvement at 6 months versus 22% on PEG**, with the benefit holding at 2 years ([Chiarioni et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16530506/)). Rao's review calls biofeedback the most effective treatment for DD, typically delivered in 4–6 one-hour sessions ([PMC4000585](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4000585/)).
- **If the cause is IBS**: low-FODMAP trial, antispasmodics, and, where available, a gut-directed CBT or hypnotherapy course — see our [low-FODMAP diet guide](/poopcheck-blog/low-fodmap-diet-guide/) and [SIBO explainer](/poopcheck-blog/sibo-explained/) for the overlap.
- **If the cause is IBD**: this is a gastroenterologist's call, with anti-inflammatory therapy aimed at the underlying disease. Treating the inflammation is what resolves the tenesmus, not symptomatic laxatives ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/24107-tenesmus)).

## Red flags — when to see a doctor this week

MedlinePlus and the Cleveland Clinic agree on the shortlist that moves this from "lifestyle project" to "book an appointment":

- **Rectal bleeding**, bright red or dark, on or in the stool
- **Unintentional weight loss**
- **Fever, nausea, or vomiting** alongside the sensation
- **Severe or new rectal pain**
- **New-onset tenesmus after age 50**, especially with any change in stool caliber
- **Persistent symptoms for more than two weeks** despite basic diet and fluid adjustments

Any of those, book the visit now and skip the extra fibre experiment ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003131.htm), [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/24107-tenesmus)).

## FAQ

**Is the feeling of incomplete evacuation always serious?**
No. Short-lived episodes after travel, a change in diet, or a stressful week are common and usually self-resolve. It becomes worth investigating when it shows up most days for more than two weeks, or when it comes with any red flag.

**What's the difference between tenesmus and constipation?**
Constipation is defined by frequency and stool form (fewer than three bowel movements a week, or hard lumpy Bristol Type 1–2 stools). Tenesmus is a sensation that can occur with constipation, with IBS, with pelvic floor problems, or with rectal inflammation — several of which the stool-frequency count will miss entirely.

**Can IBS cause the feeling of incomplete bowel movements?**
Yes, especially IBS-C and IBS-M. Visceral hypersensitivity makes a partially empty rectum feel like a full one, and the sense of incomplete evacuation is one of the Rome IV supporting features of the diagnosis.

**Does more fibre help?**
Only if the cause is slow, hard stool. If the cause is pelvic floor dyssynergia, loading more fibre and water on top of a mechanical outflow problem usually worsens bloating and straining — the fix is biofeedback, not more volume.

**What test confirms pelvic floor dyssynergia?**
Anorectal manometry plus a balloon expulsion test is the standard first-line workup, often combined with defecography when imaging will change the plan.

**Can hemorrhoids cause tenesmus?**
They can. Internal hemorrhoids and anal fissures both irritate the lower rectum and the anal canal and can keep triggering the urge sensation. The exam usually sorts this out quickly.

## The bottom line

Feeling incomplete after a bowel movement is a signal, not a diagnosis. One off day is noise. Several weeks of it is a cue to find the mechanism — hard stool, hypersensitive gut, or a pelvic floor that won't relax — because the correct fix for each one is different, and the wrong fix (more fibre on top of a mechanical bottleneck) can quietly make it worse. If any red flag is in the picture, skip the self-trial and get it checked.

Orange stool is almost always about what went in, not what went wrong. Beta-carotene from carrots, sweet potatoes, and squash; orange food dye in candy, sodas, or gelatin; and aluminum-containing antacids are the top three causes, per the [Cleveland Clinic guide to stool color](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/stool-poop-color). The exception is a shift toward pale, clay-tinted orange that persists for days — that pattern can reflect a bile flow problem and deserves a real workup, not a wellness-blog guess.

## Key takeaways

- **Diet is the top cause.** Beta-carotene-rich foods (carrots, sweet potato, squash, pumpkin) and orange food dye in processed drinks, candy, and gelatin routinely tint stool orange for 24–48 hours.
- **A handful of medications do it too.** Aluminum-hydroxide antacids (Maalox, Mylanta, Gaviscon) and the antibiotic rifampin are the most common culprits, per [Mayo Clinic drug information for rifampin](https://www.mayoclinic.org/drugs-supplements/rifampin-oral-route/description/drg-20065839).
- **Fast gut transit** after a stomach bug, high-magnesium dose, or coffee-heavy morning can leave stool orange or yellow-orange because bilirubin didn't finish converting to brown stercobilin.
- **Persistent pale orange stool** paired with jaundice, dark urine, itching, or fatty-meal pain points to a bile flow problem, not diet. That combination is a same-week call to a clinician.
- **A single orange bowel movement** with no other symptoms is almost always harmless. The signal to escalate is a **pattern lasting more than a few days** plus other red flags.

## Why stool is brown, and what turns it orange

Normal stool color is a mix of brown, yellow, and orange pigments. The brown comes from **stercobilin**, which gut bacteria make by breaking down bilirubin as stool transits the colon. The yellow-orange base underneath comes from bile and residual bilirubin itself, per the [Cleveland Clinic stool color guide](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/stool-poop-color). The longer transit takes, the more complete the conversion, and the deeper the brown.

Orange stool means one of three things has happened to that pipeline:

1. **Dietary pigment overrode it.** Beta-carotene and orange dyes deposit directly into stool faster than bile chemistry can mask them.
2. **Transit was too fast** for bilirubin to convert fully to brown stercobilin, leaving the yellow-orange base visible.
3. **Bile flow dropped**, so there's less bilirubin reaching the gut to make brown pigment in the first place. This is the concerning pattern.

The vast majority of orange stool comes from mechanism one or two, which is why the [Mayo Clinic guidance on stool color](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/diarrhea/expert-answers/stool-color/faq-20058080) frames it as: "Only rarely does stool color indicate a possibly serious intestinal condition." That framing is right, but "rarely" is not "never," and the point of this guide is telling the two apart.

## Benign causes: when orange stool is just orange

### 1. Beta-carotene foods

Beta-carotene is the orange pigment in carrots, sweet potatoes, butternut squash, pumpkin, cantaloupe, apricots, and mangoes. Eat enough of it and the pigment shows up in stool within 24–48 hours. It's the same mechanism that produces **carotenemia**, the yellow-orange skin discoloration described in the [NIH StatPearls chapter on carotenemia](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK534878/). Skin carotenemia typically shows up after weeks of high beta-carotene intake — one case series noted definite skin discoloration only in people taking 30 mg of purified beta-carotene daily, appearing 25 to 42 days after supplementation began. Stool color shifts faster than skin, because the pigment passes through in a couple of days rather than needing to load in tissue.

Turmeric and curry blends can add a yellow-orange tint through curcumin, though they usually skew more yellow than orange.

### 2. Orange food coloring

Sodas, orange sports drinks, orange gelatin, Cheetos, some breakfast cereals, and orange candy can all pass artificial orange dye straight through. Kids' snacks and Halloween candy are common triggers. Like beta-carotene, the effect clears once the food is out of your system.

### 3. Aluminum-hydroxide antacids

Antacids with aluminum hydroxide (Mylanta, Maalox, Gaviscon) frequently produce orange or lightly clay-tinted stool by binding to bile and phosphates in the gut. The effect is dose-dependent and reversible.

### 4. Rifampin and its cousins

Rifampin is a first-line tuberculosis antibiotic that famously turns almost every body fluid orange. Per [Mayo Clinic drug information for rifampin](https://www.mayoclinic.org/drugs-supplements/rifampin-oral-route/description/drg-20065839), it can color urine, stool, saliva, sputum, sweat, and tears a bright red-orange. Rifaximin (used for IBS-D and hepatic encephalopathy) and rifabutin can cause milder shifts. The color is not a sign of toxicity; it's the drug itself.

### 5. Fast gut transit

If stool moves through the large intestine too fast (think stomach bug, high-magnesium dose covered in our guide on [magnesium for constipation](/poopcheck-blog/magnesium-for-constipation/), coffee-driven morning motility, or stress), gut bacteria don't have time to fully convert bilirubin to brown stercobilin. The result is yellow-orange or greenish stool. The mechanism overlaps with green stool; the [green-stool guide](/poopcheck-blog/why-is-my-poop-green-decoding-the-secret-language-of-your-stool/) covers it in depth. If you're not sure whether the orange tint is coming from what you ate or how fast your gut is moving, [scan the stool with PoopCheck](/download/) and check whether your Bristol type has drifted toward 6 or 7 at the same time. A soft-and-orange combination usually means transit, not diet.

### 6. Escolar and a few unusual foods

Escolar (a tropical fish sometimes marketed as "white tuna") contains an indigestible wax ester that can pass through the gut and produce orange, oily, floating stool. It's a specific and well-documented food effect, not a disease.

## Concerning causes: when "orange" is really pale

The one pattern worth taking seriously is orange stool that shifts pale over days, the color that clinicians call **acholic** or **clay-colored**. Per the [Cleveland Clinic guide to pale and clay-colored stool](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/clay-colored-or-pale-stool), pale stool reflects reduced bile pigment reaching the gut. Without bile, stool loses its brown notes and drops back to whatever pale orange, tan, or clay tint remains underneath.

Common causes include:

- **Gallstones lodged in the common bile duct** (choledocholithiasis)
- **Bile duct strictures** from prior surgery, inflammation, or scarring
- **Hepatitis** (viral, alcoholic, or drug-induced) reducing bile production
- **Cirrhosis** or advanced fatty liver disease
- **Primary biliary cholangitis** and **primary sclerosing cholangitis**
- **Pancreatic head tumors** compressing the bile duct
- **Cystic fibrosis** in children

The classic presentation is pale stool paired with **dark tea-colored urine**, **yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice)**, and sometimes **itching** or **upper-right abdominal pain**. That combination is a same-day call to a clinician. Pale orange or clay-tinted stool without the other signs is much less urgent, but still worth a phone call if it lasts more than a week.

## Red flags: when to see a doctor

An occasional orange bowel movement after a carrot-heavy salad or a course of antacids is not a symptom. Book an appointment if any of the following apply:

- Pale, greasy, or clay-tinted stool lasting **more than one to two weeks**
- **Jaundice** (yellow skin or eye-whites) or **dark tea-colored urine**
- **Itching** without a rash (a classic sign of bile buildup)
- **Upper-right abdominal pain**, especially after fatty meals
- **Unintentional weight loss** with any persistent stool color change
- **Fever plus abdominal pain** (possible cholangitis, a medical emergency)
- A child with **failure to thrive** and persistently pale stool

A single orange stool with no other symptoms doesn't qualify. The decision point is **pattern plus accompanying signs**, not color alone. ## When orange stool is *probably* nothing

It's reasonable to watch and wait if **all** of the following are true:

- You had a recent high-beta-carotene meal, an orange-dyed drink or candy, an antacid, or a course of rifampin
- The orange color resolves within two to three days
- Stool consistency is roughly normal (Bristol 3–5), not greasy, oily, or clay-textured
- You feel otherwise fine: no jaundice, no dark urine, no itching, no fatty-meal pain, no weight loss

If a couple of days pass and color is still off, or if any red-flag symptom shows up, escalate.

## FAQ

**Is orange poop always caused by diet?**
No, but diet is the top cause. Beta-carotene foods (carrots, sweet potato, squash), orange food dye, aluminum-containing antacids, and the antibiotic rifampin are the four most common triggers. Fast gut transit after a stomach bug or a magnesium dose can also produce orange or yellow-orange stool. Persistent pale orange stool combined with jaundice or dark urine points instead to a bile flow issue and deserves a same-week clinician visit.

**How long does orange stool from carrots or sweet potato last?**
Usually 24 to 48 hours after the meal, sometimes up to three days if you've been eating a lot of beta-carotene over a stretch. The color clears as the pigment leaves your system. Skin carotenemia, by contrast, takes weeks to appear and weeks to clear.

**Can antacids make my poop orange?**
Yes. Antacids containing aluminum hydroxide (Mylanta, Maalox, Gaviscon) frequently cause orange or lightly pale stool by binding to bile in the gut. It's dose-dependent and reversible. If the shade tips toward clay-colored and stays there after you stop the antacid, that's worth mentioning to your doctor.

**Should I worry if my orange stool is also greasy or floating?**
Yes, especially if it lasts more than a week. Greasy, oily, foul-smelling stool that floats and is hard to flush suggests **steatorrhea** (unabsorbed dietary fat), which reflects a pancreatic, biliary, or small-bowel malabsorption problem. Our [dedicated yellow-stool guide](/poopcheck-blog/yellow-stool-causes/) breaks down the workup in detail; the same rules apply when the color skews orange.

**Is orange diarrhea an emergency?**
Usually not on its own. Orange diarrhea most often reflects fast gut transit (the same mechanism behind green diarrhea) combined with something orange in your recent diet or medications. Escalate if the diarrhea lasts more than 48–72 hours, is bloody, comes with a high fever, causes dehydration, or occurs alongside jaundice or severe abdominal pain.

**Can babies have orange poop?**
Yes, and it's usually normal. Breastfed babies routinely produce mustard-yellow to orange, seedy, loose stool. Formula-fed infants trend tan to light brown, and introducing orange purees (carrot, sweet potato, squash) around six months often produces bright orange stool for a day or two. Persistently pale or clay-colored stool in an infant, especially with jaundice beyond the newborn period, needs pediatric evaluation to rule out biliary atresia.

## The bottom line

Most orange stool is benign: carrots, squash, orange dye, antacids, rifampin, or a fast-transit day after a stomach bug. The pattern that matters is pale orange or clay-tinted stool that lasts more than a week and comes with jaundice, dark urine, itching, or fatty-meal pain — those together point to a bile flow problem that deserves a real workup. A single orange bowel movement with no other symptoms is almost never worth a doctor's visit; a persistent shift with red-flag signs almost always is. A short dated photo log is the most useful artifact you can bring to that appointment.
